Baby Food Safety

Feeding a baby safely is about more than choosing healthy foods. Babies are more vulnerable to food poisoning and choking than adults, and their kidneys cannot handle salt the way ours can. The rules are straightforward, but they are worth knowing before the first spoonful: good hygiene, safe preparation, careful storage, and knowing which foods to avoid. This guide brings all the essential safety rules together in one place.

Preventing choking

Always sit your baby upright in a highchair and stay with them while they eat. Cut food into finger-length strips for younger babies and small pieces for older ones, and cut round foods like grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways into quarters. Avoid whole nuts, popcorn, hard sweets, raw hard vegetables, and large chunks of food. Do not let your baby eat while lying down, walking, or playing. Learn basic infant first aid, including what to do if a baby chokes, before starting solids. Gagging is common and noisy; choking is silent and needs immediate help.

Hygiene in the kitchen

Wash your hands and your baby's hands before preparing and eating food. Wash fruit and vegetables well, and use separate chopping boards for raw meat and for foods your baby will eat. Cook meat, fish, and eggs thoroughly, and check that there is no pink meat and no runny egg white in food for young babies. Clean highchair trays, spoons, and bowls with hot soapy water. Wash dummies and teething toys regularly. Good kitchen hygiene is the simplest way to prevent food poisoning, which can be serious for a baby.

Foods to avoid

Do not give honey before twelve months because of the risk of infant botulism. Avoid added salt and sugar in baby food, and do not offer salty foods such as bacon, processed cheese, or ready meals. Whole cow's milk should not be a main drink before twelve months. Avoid raw or undercooked meat, fish, and eggs, and unpasteurised milk and soft cheeses. Avoid fruit juice, fizzy drinks, and flavoured milk. Check labels for added salt and sugar, especially in sauces, bread, and breakfast cereals.

Storing and reheating safely

Cool cooked food quickly, within two hours, and store it in the fridge for no more than forty-eight hours. Freeze food you will not use within two days, and label it with the date. Defrost in the fridge overnight, never at room temperature. Reheat until steaming hot all the way through, then cool to eating temperature and test it on your wrist. Reheat food only once, and throw away anything your baby has eaten from. Never taste from your baby's spoon and put it back, because that spreads bacteria.

Teaching others the rules

Other people who care for your baby need to know the safety rules too, so share them clearly. Write a short note for grandparents, nursery staff, and babysitters, listing the foods to avoid, how to cut food safely, and what to do if your baby chokes. Show them how to test food temperature and how to store leftovers. Keep the note short and practical, and update it as your baby grows. Most carers are glad to have clear guidance, and it gives you peace of mind when you are not there. A shared understanding of the rules keeps your baby safe wherever they eat.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do if my baby chokes?

Stay calm, call for help, and give up to five back blows followed by up to five chest thrusts. Learn infant first aid before starting solids so you know exactly what to do.

Can babies eat runny eggs?

From six months, eggs should be cooked until both the white and yolk are solid. Well-cooked eggs are safe and nutritious.

How can I tell if baby food has gone off?

Smell it and check the date. If it has been in the fridge for more than 48 hours, or you are unsure, throw it away.

Important: every baby is different. Always follow your pediatrician's advice on when to start solids, which foods to offer, and how to introduce allergens. The guidance on this site is general information, not medical advice.
