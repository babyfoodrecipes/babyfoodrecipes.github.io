Iron-Rich Baby Foods

Iron is one of the most important nutrients in a baby's diet after six months. Babies are born with iron stores, but those stores begin to run low at around six months, just as they start eating solids. Iron supports brain development and helps carry oxygen in the blood, and a shortage can affect learning and growth. The good news is that many everyday foods are rich in iron, and a few simple habits can help your baby absorb it well. This guide lists the best iron-rich foods and explains how to build them into daily meals.

Why iron matters at six months

A baby's iron stores from birth usually last for about six months. After that, breast milk alone does not provide enough iron, because it contains only small amounts, so solid foods must make up the difference. Formula is usually iron-fortified, but babies on formula also benefit from iron-rich solids. Low iron can cause tiredness, pale skin, poor appetite, and slower development, and it is one of the most common nutrient deficiencies in young children. Offering iron-rich foods every day from the start of weaning is the simplest way to prevent problems.

The best iron-rich foods for babies

Meat is one of the best sources: well-cooked beef, lamb, chicken, and pork all provide iron that the body absorbs easily. Fish, especially oily fish like salmon and sardines, also contains iron. Eggs are a good source, particularly the yolk. Plant sources include lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu, and dark green vegetables like spinach and broccoli. Iron-fortified baby cereals and porridge oats are useful everyday sources. Dried fruit such as apricots contains iron too, but it is sticky and best offered with meals rather than as a snack.

Helping your baby absorb iron

Vitamin C helps the body absorb iron from plant foods, so pair iron-rich foods with fruit or vegetables rich in vitamin C. Lentils with peppers, beans with tomatoes, or porridge with berries are all good combinations. Avoid offering tea with meals, as it blocks iron absorption, and do not give large amounts of cow's milk as a drink before twelve months, because it is low in iron and can reduce appetite for iron-rich foods. Cooking in a cast iron pan can also add small amounts of iron to food, although this is a bonus rather than a main strategy.

A day of iron-rich meals

A simple iron-rich day might start with iron-fortified porridge with mashed berries, followed by lentil and carrot puree at lunch, and chicken and sweet potato mash at dinner. Snacks can include a strip of omelette or a small piece of cheese. Over a week, aim to offer meat or fish most days, plus lentils or beans several times a week, and an iron-fortified cereal most mornings. Variety matters more than any single meal: a mix of animal and plant sources across the week covers your baby's needs well.

Iron across the week

Think about iron across the whole week rather than in a single meal. A good week includes meat or fish on most days, lentils or beans two or three times, and an iron-fortified cereal most mornings. Monday might be chicken and sweet potato, Tuesday lentil soup, Wednesday beef stew, Thursday salmon and broccoli, and Friday egg and vegetable frittata. Add a vitamin C food to each meal, such as peppers, tomatoes, or berries, to help iron absorption. Variety matters more than any single meal, and a simple weekly plan like this makes it easy to see that your baby is getting enough iron.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much iron does a baby need each day?

Babies aged seven to twelve months need about 11 milligrams of iron a day. A varied diet with meat, lentils, and fortified cereals usually provides this.

Can a vegetarian baby get enough iron?

Yes, with careful planning. Lentils, beans, tofu, fortified cereals, and dark green vegetables provide iron, especially when paired with vitamin C foods.

Should I give my baby an iron supplement?

Only if your doctor recommends it. Most babies get enough iron from food, and supplements can cause constipation. Ask your pediatrician if you are concerned.

Important: every baby is different. Always follow your pediatrician's advice on when to start solids, which foods to offer, and how to introduce allergens. The guidance on this site is general information, not medical advice.
