Finger Foods

Finger foods are soft pieces of food your baby can pick up and feed to themselves. They usually appear at around seven to eight months, once a baby can sit up and grasp things, and they play a big part in learning to chew, developing hand-eye coordination, and building confidence with food. The key is choosing foods that are soft enough to squash between your fingers and cutting them into shapes that are easy to hold. This guide explains when to start, what to offer, and how to keep finger-food time safe and enjoyable.

When to start finger foods

Most babies are ready for finger foods at around seven to eight months, once they can sit upright with little support and can pick things up with their whole hand. They do not need teeth: gums are surprisingly strong and can mash soft food well. Start alongside purees and mashes, not instead of them, so your baby still gets enough nutrition while they practise. Early signs of readiness include reaching for food on your plate, picking up small objects, and moving food from hand to mouth. If your baby is not interested yet, wait a week and try again.

Safe first finger foods

Good first choices are foods that are soft and easy to grip: steamed carrot sticks, broccoli florets with a stalk handle, strips of omelette, soft toast fingers, cooked pasta pieces, banana cut into fingers, avocado slices rolled in ground oats to stop slipping, and soft pieces of cheese. Cooked sweet potato wedges, soft pear slices, and well-cooked green beans also work well. Cut food into finger-length strips rather than small cubes, because strips are easier for a small hand to hold and bite. Always serve food at room temperature or just warm.

Foods to avoid

Some foods are dangerous for babies. Never offer whole grapes, whole cherry tomatoes, whole nuts, popcorn, hard raw carrot or apple, or large chunks of meat or cheese, because these are choking hazards. Cut grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways into quarters, grate hard fruits or cook them soft, and avoid anything small, round, and hard. Skip added salt and sugar, honey before twelve months, and processed snacks like crisps. Avoid sticky foods like large spoonfuls of peanut butter, which can block the airway; thinly spread nut butter is fine once nuts have been introduced safely.

Making finger-food time work

Offer finger foods when your baby is alert and hungry but not too tired, and always stay with them while they eat. Sit them upright in a highchair and let them explore at their own pace; mess is part of learning. Gagging is common at first and is different from choking: a gagging baby makes noise and works the food forward, while a choking baby is silent and cannot breathe. Learn basic infant first aid before starting solids so you know what to do. Offer water in a small open cup or sippy cup with meals, and keep sessions relaxed and short.

Building confidence with finger foods

Confidence grows with practice, so offer finger foods every day, even if most of it ends up on the floor. Start with one or two pieces on the tray and add more once your baby has tried them. Sit with your baby, eat the same food yourself, and show them how to pick it up and bite it. Praise every attempt, even if the food is dropped or squashed, because exploration is part of learning. Avoid taking food away quickly or wiping your baby's hands after every bite, because mess is normal. Within a few weeks, most babies become much more skilled and much less messy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between gagging and choking?

Gagging is noisy and normal: the baby coughs or retches and moves food forward. Choking is silent, the baby cannot breathe or cry, and needs immediate first aid. Learn the difference before starting solids.

Can babies eat finger foods without teeth?

Yes. Babies mash soft food with their gums, which are strong enough for steamed vegetables, soft fruit, pasta, and toast fingers.

How many finger foods should I offer at once?

Start with one or two pieces on the tray. Too many pieces can overwhelm a baby. Add more once they have eaten or dropped the first ones.

Important: every baby is different. Always follow your pediatrician's advice on when to start solids, which foods to offer, and how to introduce allergens. The guidance on this site is general information, not medical advice.
