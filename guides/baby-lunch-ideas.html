Baby Lunch Ideas

Lunch is often the meal where babies try new savoury flavours and start eating more like the rest of the family. A good baby lunch includes a starchy food, a protein, and a vegetable, but it does not need to be complicated: leftovers, simple soups, and finger foods all count. This guide gives lunch ideas for each stage of weaning, tips for using leftovers safely, and ways to keep lunch quick on busy days.

Lunches for six to eight months

At this stage lunch is a small portion of puree or mash, offered after a milk feed. Good choices are carrot and lentil puree, sweet potato and apple mash, or pea and potato puree. You can also offer soft finger foods alongside the puree, such as a steamed broccoli floret or a strip of omelette, so your baby starts practising self-feeding. Keep flavours mild and textures smooth, and introduce one new ingredient at a time. A lunch of two or three tablespoons is plenty at this age.

Lunches for eight to ten months

Babies this age can manage thicker mashes and more finger foods. Try lentil and vegetable soup blended thick, pasta pieces with a mild tomato sauce, or mashed chickpeas with avocado. Soft sandwiches made with thin bread, cream cheese, and mashed banana or avocado work well. Offer a soft fruit after the main course, such as pear slices or berries cut in half. This is a good time to introduce mild herbs and spices like basil, cinnamon, and cumin, which build your baby's flavour range.

Lunches for ten to twelve months

By now, lunch can be a small portion of the family meal, chopped to a safe size. Pasta with vegetables and cheese, rice with lentils and soft vegetables, or a small portion of shepherd's pie all work well. Sandwiches, wraps cut into strips, and small pieces of quiche are handy for outings. Include a protein at lunch every day, whether that is meat, fish, egg, beans, or lentils. Offer water in a cup with the meal, and keep the atmosphere relaxed so your baby enjoys eating.

Using leftovers and eating out

Leftovers make the easiest baby lunches. Cook extra vegetables, pasta, or rice at dinner and save a portion for lunch the next day, stored in the fridge and reheated until steaming hot. When eating out, choose simple foods: plain pasta, steamed vegetables, bread, and fruit are usually available. Avoid salty restaurant food, and ask for dishes without added salt where you can. Pack a small lunchbox with a sandwich, fruit, and a yoghurt for days out, and keep it cool with an ice pack.

Planning lunch for the week

Planning lunch for the week saves time and reduces stress. On Sunday, look at what you will cook for dinner during the week and note which dishes will give good leftovers for lunch, such as pasta, stew, or roast vegetables. Cook a batch of lentil soup and a batch of finger foods, and portion them into small containers for the fridge and freezer. Keep a list on the fridge of what is available, so you can put lunch together in minutes. Pack a lunchbox the night before on days when you will be out, with a sandwich, fruit, and yoghurt, and keep it cool with an ice pack.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should my baby eat at lunch?

At six months, just a few spoonfuls. By twelve months, most babies eat around half to three-quarters of a small bowl, plus a finger food and some fruit.

Can I give my baby leftovers?

Yes, if they were stored properly in the fridge and used within 48 hours, and reheated until steaming hot. Never reheat leftovers more than once.

Should lunch include a pudding?

A piece of fruit or a small yoghurt is a fine finish. There is no need for sweet puddings or biscuits at this age.

Important: every baby is different. Always follow your pediatrician's advice on when to start solids, which foods to offer, and how to introduce allergens. The guidance on this site is general information, not medical advice.
