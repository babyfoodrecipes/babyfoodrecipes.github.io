Introducing Allergens

Introducing allergens is one of the most important parts of starting solids. Research now shows that introducing common allergenic foods early, at around six months, and then offering them regularly, can reduce the chance of a food allergy developing. The nine common allergens are egg, peanut, tree nuts, cow's milk, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish, and sesame. This guide explains when and how to introduce each one, how to do it safely, and what signs of a reaction to watch for.

When to introduce allergens

Most guidance now recommends introducing allergens at around six months, once your baby has started solids, rather than delaying them. There is no benefit in waiting until after the first birthday. Introduce one new allergen at a time, a few days apart, so that if a reaction happens you know which food caused it. Start with a tiny amount, such as a quarter of a teaspoon, and if there is no reaction, gradually increase the amount over the next few days. Once an allergen has been introduced safely, keep offering it regularly, a few times a week, because regular exposure is part of building tolerance.

How to introduce each allergen

Egg can be offered well cooked, as scrambled egg or in an omelette. Peanut should never be given whole; use smooth peanut butter thinned with a little water or breast milk, or peanut flour mixed into porridge. Tree nuts can be offered as smooth nut butter, never whole. Cow's milk is fine in cooked food from six months, but not as a main drink until twelve months. Wheat can come from porridge, pasta, or bread. Soy can be introduced through tofu or soy yoghurt. Fish and shellfish should be well cooked and checked for bones. Sesame can be offered as tahini thinned with water.

Signs of a reaction

Most reactions are mild and appear within minutes to two hours of eating. Watch for a rash or hives, swelling of the lips, face, or tongue, vomiting, diarrhoea, or a sudden change in behaviour such as unusual sleepiness or distress. A severe reaction, called anaphylaxis, is rare but serious: it involves difficulty breathing, swelling of the throat, or collapse. If you see those signs, call emergency services immediately. Mild reactions should still be reported to your pediatrician, who can advise whether to keep offering the food or to avoid it and seek allergy testing.

If your baby has eczema or a family history of allergy

Babies with moderate to severe eczema or an existing food allergy are at higher risk of developing another allergy, so talk to your pediatrician before introducing allergens. They may suggest allergy testing first, or introducing the food under supervision. Do not delay introducing allergens on your own because of family history alone, but do get advice if your baby already has an allergy or eczema that is hard to control. Keep a simple food diary during weaning so you can show your doctor exactly what was eaten and when, if any reaction occurs.

Keeping a food diary

A simple food diary is very useful during weaning. Write down the date, the food you offered, and how much your baby ate, and note any reaction, such as a rash, vomiting, or unusual fussiness, with the time it appeared. A notebook on the kitchen counter is enough, or you can use a notes app on your phone. The diary helps you remember which allergens you have introduced and which are still to come, and it gives your pediatrician clear information if a reaction happens. Keep the diary for the first few months of weaning, until all nine common allergens have been introduced and are being offered regularly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I introduce peanut before other allergens?

Order does not matter much. Peanut and egg are the most important to introduce early, but all nine should be introduced during the first year.

Can I give my baby whole nuts?

No. Whole nuts are a choking hazard and should not be given before age five. Use smooth nut butters thinned with water or milk instead.

How often should I offer an allergen once introduced?

Regularly, a few times a week. Tolerance builds with repeated exposure, so do not introduce an allergen once and then stop offering it.

Important: every baby is different. Always follow your pediatrician's advice on when to start solids, which foods to offer, and how to introduce allergens. The guidance on this site is general information, not medical advice.
